# Completing the Course

This page describes what is required to pass **ISEP1002 Programming 2** and how the final grade is determined.

## Requirements in Brief

To pass the course, you must complete **all three** of the following:

1. **Exercises:** earn at least 50% of the core exercise points in **every** part (Parts 1–11).
2. **Project assignment:** complete the [project assignment](./project-assignment.md) (Parts 9–12) in a pair.
3. **Reflection:** submit the individual reflection at the end of the project (Part 12).

These are all assessed on a pass/fail basis; they are required for passing, but they do not determine the grade.

The final grade (0–5) comes from **weekly oral exams** and the **project grade**, as described below. Your grade is based on what you can explain live about your own code, not on what you submit.

The final deadline for completing all of the above is announced on the [front page](./index.md) and in TIM.

## The Friday Oral

Every Friday during the theory weeks, each student has a **10-minute oral exam** with the responsible teacher.

1. The teacher picks one exercise from your own code for that week.
2. You say which level you are going for.
3. The teacher asks questions at that level and moves down if needed.
4. The level you reach is your grade for that week.

| Grade | Level | What you do | Typical question |
| ----- | ----- | ----------- | ---------------- |
| 1 | Explain | Describe what the code does | "Walk me through what happens when this line runs." |
| 3 | Modify | Predict and make a small change | "What happens if I change this? Make the change now." |
| 5 | Transfer | Justify the design, handle a new situation | "Why this design and not another? What if the requirement changes?" |

Grades 2 and 4 are for in between. Grade 0 means nothing was shown.

**Week 1 is practice.** It runs exactly like the other orals, but no grade is recorded. You learn the format without pressure.

## Growth Rule

No grade is final before the course ends.

- From week 3, each Friday you may retry **one** earlier graded week. The best result counts.
- Retries stay open until the last Friday of the course.

A weak week costs nothing. Come back when the topic has clicked.

## Final Grade

Every graded week counts the same. The final grade is the **average of six grades, rounded**:

- three oral grades (weeks 2, 3 and 4),
- the project grade, counted three times (weeks 5–7).

Example: orals 3, 5, 5 and project grade 4 gives (3 + 5 + 5 + 4 + 4 + 4) / 6 = 4.2, so the final grade is **4**.

## Exercises

Each part of the material ends with a set of exercises that you complete and submit in [TIM](https://tim.jyu.fi/view/kurssit/it/iseai/26-27/programming2). Every exercise is worth one point.

Exercises come in two kinds:

- **Core exercises** make up the "100%" of each part.
- **Bonus exercises** (marked with <i class="bi bi-stars jyu-gold"></i>) are optional. Their points are added to your total, so with bonus exercises your percentage for a part can exceed 100%.

Exercise points do not determine the grade, but they matter twice over: you must reach the per-part minimum to pass the course, and the Friday oral is always about **your own exercise code** — an exercise you did yourself is your best preparation.

### Minimum per Part

You must earn **at least 50% of the core points in every part** from Part 1 to Part 11. Bonus points count towards this minimum too: for example, in Part 1 you need 3 points, which you may collect from any combination of core and bonus exercises.

The per-part minimum ensures that you cover every topic of the course, including the JavaFX parts and the project milestones in Parts 9–11, all of which you will need for the project assignment.

## Project Assignment

The [project assignment](./project-assignment.md) is a JavaFX application that you build **in a pair** during Parts 9–12 (weeks 5–7).

- **Weeks 5 and 6:** each pair has a Friday checkpoint with the teacher. Feedback only, no grade.
- **Week 7:** another pair adds one feature to your code and writes half a page on how easy the code was to work in (the *handoff*). Then each student has a final oral about the project.

The project gets **one grade, 0–5**, from three questions:

| Question | Who is judged |
| -------- | ------------- |
| Does it work? | The pair |
| Could another pair build in it? (The handoff shows it.) | The pair |
| Can you explain and change it? | Each student individually |

Because the third question is individual, partners can end up one grade apart.

The [technical requirements](./project-assignment.md#technical-requirements-and-assessment) on the project page define the baseline for "does it work". A project that does not yet meet them can be revised based on the teacher's feedback before the final oral.

## Reflection

At the end of the project (Part 12) you write a short, individual reflection on your work. The purpose is not to grade your project again, but to help you learn from the process: what worked, what did not, and what you would do differently next time. Each member of the pair writes their own reflection.

The reflection is submitted in TIM as plain text.

## Academic Integrity and the Use of AI

You are responsible for all the work you submit. Copying, or presenting someone else's work as your own, is prohibited. Working together is allowed, but every member of the group must contribute, understand what they have done and be able to explain it. Any part of a submission produced as group work must be clearly marked, for example in code comments.

The course follows the [University of Jyväskylä's guidelines on the use of AI-based applications in studies](https://www.jyu.fi/en/for-students/instructions-for-bachelors-and-masters-students/regulations-and-directives-guiding-studies/using-ai-based-applications-in-studies-jyus-instructions-and-guidelines). Read them carefully if you intend to use AI tools in your studies. The guidelines below complement them.

Using generative AI to produce ready-made answers to exercises or project code is prohibited. Note that the grading model enforces this by itself: your grade comes from the Friday orals, and code you cannot explain and change live earns nothing.

You may use generative AI as a learning aid: for example, to explain concepts, to help you understand an assignment, to interpret error messages, or to explain the examples in the material and produce new ones. Keep in mind that AI tools readily produce complete solutions, so your prompt should typically make it clear that you want to understand the topic and *not* receive a direct solution.

<details><summary>Examples of acceptable prompts</summary>

- *What does encapsulation mean and how does it relate to object-oriented programming?*
- *My code gives the error message 'X'. Explain what the message means and what could cause it.*
- *My course has the following assignment: 'Write a function that computes the Fibonacci sequence up to the n-th term.' How could I approach solving it?*

</details>

<details><summary>Examples of prohibited prompts</summary>

- *Write a function that computes the Fibonacci sequence up to the n-th term.*
- *What is the correct code for exercise X?*
- *Make me a JavaFX application for tracking expenses and income.*
- *Add a new feature to my JavaFX application that does X.*

</details>

The use of any AI tools during the oral exams, project checkpoints and similar assessment situations is strictly prohibited.

Suspected cases of academic misconduct are handled according to the [University of Jyväskylä's regulations on handling cases of fraud](https://www.jyu.fi/en/for-students/instructions-for-bachelors-and-masters-students/regulations-and-directives-guiding-studies/dealing-with-academic-misconduct).
